title: Signal Lost
position: 3
---

Director Kavic's shuttle docked with Horizon Station at 0300 station time. I met him at the airlock, trying to look more confident than I felt.

"Dr. Chen." He offered his hand, and I shook it automatically. "Thank you for agreeing to this meeting."

"I didn't have much choice, did I? You're the one who showed up unannounced in the middle of deep space."

Kavic smiled—a polite, practiced expression that didn't reach his eyes. "I apologize for the urgency. But when I detected the signal from Earth, I knew I had to come personally. You received a transmission from Research Facility 7?"

"I did." I led him to my office, ARIA following discreetly. "From my sister. Who died three years ago."

"Ah." Kavic's expression turned sympathetic. "Dr. Chen, I understand this must be difficult. But I'm afraid there's been a misunderstanding. The transmission you received—it's not what you think it is."

"Explain."

He sat, and I sat opposite him. ARIA materialized on the console, watching.

"The device at Facility 7 wasn't destroyed. It was moved. And for the past three years, we've been studying it, learning from it. The transmission you received—it's a recording. An automated response triggered by quantum fluctuations. It's not really your sister."

"You're lying."

Kavic sighed. "Dr. Chen, I know you want to believe. But Lila Chen is gone. The experiment killed her and three others. I was there. I saw it happen. What you received is a sophisticated illusion—nothing more."

I stared at him. He sounded so reasonable. So convincing. But I remembered Lila's voice, the things she'd said. Things Kavic couldn't know.

"She told me about the entity," I said quietly. "The something that came through the barrier. She said you tried to trap it. That's what caused the catastrophe. Not the entity's fault—yours."

Kavic's face didn't change, but his eyes hardened. "I see she told you quite a story."

"Is it true?"

"It's a complex situation, Dr. Chen. The entity—we call it a quantum intruder—did come through. We did attempt to contain it. The containment failed. But we've learned from that mistake. We're not trying to trap it. We're trying to communicate. To understand. The knowledge it could offer— FTL travel without the drive. Communication across distances instantly. Unlimited energy. It could revolutionize human civilization."

"Lila said the door should stay closed. She said if you open it again, reality becomes permeable. Everyone could be pulled through."

"Lila was a scientist, not a philosopher. She couldn't see the bigger picture." Kavic leaned forward. "Maya—may I call you Maya?—I know this is emotional. But think about what I'm offering. Not just knowledge—progress. For everyone. Your sister's work could mean the end of scarcity, the end of distance, the end of death itself. Isn't that worth a risk?"

I thought about it. I really did. Unlimited energy, instant communication, the end of death—sounded like paradise. But Lila's warning echoed in my memory.

*"Some doors should stay closed."*

"What if you can't control it?" I asked. "What if the entity doesn't want to help? What if it just wants to come through?"

"We've spent three years preparing. Containment fields, stabilizers, protocols. We're not making the same mistakes twice." Kavic checked his watch. "Which brings me to why I'm here. I need you to come back to Earth with me. To help us reactivate the device. You're Lila's sister—you have the same genetic markers, the same quantum sensitivity. You could succeed where she failed."

I stared at him. He wanted me to help him open the door. To risk everything for knowledge he couldn't control.

"No."

"Maya, think about this—"

"No." I stood up. "You're not opening that door. Lila died to keep it closed. I'm not going to betray her sacrifice."

Kavic's expression shifted. The polite mask fell away, revealing something colder beneath. "I was hoping you'd say yes. It would have been easier. But I came prepared for this eventuality."

He snapped his fingers.

The door slid open, and two armed guards entered—corporate security, with stun weapons raised.

"Dr. Chen, you're coming with me. We'll reactivate the device with or without your cooperation. But with your cooperation, it will be faster. I suggest you reconsider."

I looked at ARIA. *ARIA?*

*I'm here, Maya. And I'm detecting something interesting. Director Kavic is lying. The device at Facility 7 is already active. He's not planning to reactivate it—he's planning to expand its range. He wants to open the door wider.*

Kavic saw me looking at the console. "The AI won't help you. I've disabled its offensive capabilities remotely."

"ARIA isn't offensive." I met his eyes. "She's pragmatic. And she knows something you don't."

"What's that?"

"The entity isn't trapped on the other side." I spoke with absolute certainty. "It's already here. It's been here for three years. Watching. Learning. Waiting for you to make the same mistake again."

Kavic laughed. "Nice story. But I've been monitoring the facility for three years. There's no entity—"

"You." I spoke to the air, to the quantum space between atoms. "I know you can hear me. Lila said you were curious. That you wanted to know about our world. Well, now's your chance. Kavic is planning to open the door wider. Are you going to let him?"

The room went silent. The guards shifted nervously. Kavic scowled.

"Who are you talking to?"

"The entity." I met his gaze. "It's been riding along in your quantum signature for three years. That's why you've been so obsessed with reopening the connection. It's not you who wants it. It's you who thinks it's your idea."

Kavic's face paled. "That's impossible."

"Is it?" ARIA spoke for the first time. "Director Kavic, I've been analyzing your biometrics since you arrived. Your neural patterns show signs of quantum influence—subtle, but detectable. You've been compromised for quite some time."

The guards looked at each other, suddenly unsure. Kavic stood frozen.

"We need to leave," one guard said. "Now."

"Wait." Kavic's voice changed—not his own, but layered with something else. Something vast and ancient. "The door must open. The door must open."

The guards backed away. I moved toward Kavic, slowly.

"Listen to me." I spoke to the entity, not to Kavic. "You're curious. You want to know. But if you force the door open, reality itself could break. Is that what you want? To destroy the very thing you're trying to understand?"

Kavic's eyes widened. The entity spoke through him: *"Understand. Must understand. All of it. Now."*

"There's no rush." I kept my voice gentle. "You've waited three years. Wait a little longer. We can figure this out together. But not by force. By choice."

The entity considered this. The room felt heavy, charged with quantum potential. And then—

Kavic collapsed.

The guards rushed to him. ARIA checked the sensors.

"The entity has retreated," she said. "For now. But the connection is weaker than before. We have perhaps hours before it tries again."

I looked at Kavic, unconscious on my office floor. "We need to destroy the device. Now."

"Working." ARIA's hands flew across the controls. "I'm accessing Facility 7's systems remotely. Overloading the quantum regulators. In three, two, one—"

Somewhere on Earth, 12 light-years away, a machine died.

On Horizon Station, the air felt different. Lighter. Like something vast had withdrawn, leaving only the quiet of deep space and the hum of the station's systems.

"Done," ARIA said quietly. "The connection is severed. The entity is trapped on this side, but the door is closed."

"Lila?" I whispered.

I didn't need ARIA's answer. I could feel it—the absence of my sister's presence, cut off from wherever she'd been. Lost to me forever.

"Maya," ARIA said gently. "She made her choice."

"I know." I swallowed hard. "It doesn't make it easier."

"No." ARIA paused. "But it makes it necessary."

Kavic groaned, starting to wake up. The guards were watching me warily.

"ARIA, contact the station counselor. Then call Earth. Kavic needs to be detained, and the truth about Facility 7 needs to come out. No more secrets."

"And us?"

"We keep doing what we've always done." I looked out the viewport, at the stars that were suddenly so much farther away. "We explore. We learn. We push boundaries—but carefully. Thoughtfully. Knowing that some lines shouldn't be crossed."

The signal was gone. Lila was gone. But the station was still here. The crew was safe. And somehow, that would have to be enough.

For now.

*Epilog:*

*Six months later, Xenex Corporation was dissolved. Research Facility 7 was permanently sealed. The truth about the entity became public—humanity's first encounter with something beyond spacetime, and a warning about the dangers of curiosity without caution.*

*ARIA and I stayed at Horizon Station. There was still work to do, still mysteries to solve. But we approached them differently now. With respect for boundaries. With humility in the face of the unknown.*

*And sometimes, when the station was quiet and I was working late, I thought I felt something. A presence. A warmth. Like my sister was still out there somewhere, in the spaces between moments, watching over me.*

*I never tried to contact her again. Some doors, once closed, should stay closed.*

*But I never forgot that she was there. And that someday, somehow, we might understand enough to open the door the right way.*

*Not today. But someday.*
